Visa applications, mortgages, benefits claims, job applications — the forms long enough to need a break, and unforgiving enough to lose everything when you take one. Form For Later keeps what you have already typed, on your own device, and puts it back in one click.

How it works

Three steps, and the middle one is leaving.

01

Save what is on screen

Click the toolbar icon, or press the shortcut. It reads the answers already filled in and works out what each one is called from the label you can see — not from whatever the site named the field.

02

Close the tab. Sleep on it.

Everything is written to this device and nowhere else. Take an hour or take a fortnight; a timed-out session stops being the thing that decides when you finish.

03

Put it back in one click

Open the form again and restore. It tells you how many answers landed and how many it matched by their label rather than exactly, because those are the ones worth a second look.

The part nothing else does

Hand someone an unfinished form without handing over all of it.

Half of finishing a hard form is somebody else — an accountant, a caseworker, a relative who has done this before. The usual options are emailing your answers around or reading them down a phone line.

Instead, send a link. It is encrypted in your browser before it exists, it stops working on a date you choose, and you decide field by field what travels. Anything you hold back is removed before encryption — not hidden behind a button on the other end.

  • Encrypted where you are. AES-256-GCM, with a key that lives in the link and never reaches any server.
  • It expires. One hour, a day, or a week — and optionally deletes itself the first time it is opened.
  • They need nothing. No account, no install. A link and a browser.
  • Passwords cannot be in it. They were never saved in the first place, so there is nothing to leak.

When it earns its place

Three ordinary afternoons.

The twelfth job application

The same eight answers, retyped into a different applicant tracking system, with three custom questions bolted on the end. Save one, and the next one starts most of the way finished.

A form on behalf of a parent

You fill in what you know, send them the rest to check, and hold back the bits that are not yours to forward. They read it in a browser, with no account to create.

An application across two evenings

Forty minutes on Tuesday, the rest on Thursday. The portal logs you out somewhere in between, as portals do, and it costs you a click rather than the forty minutes.

Privacy

Worth reading before you install anything that can see what you type.

The claims below are the ones the product is actually built on, and the privacy policy names the file and function behind each of them so you can check rather than trust.

  • Nothing leaves the device

    Your answers are written to your browser’s own storage on this machine. There is no copy on a server of ours, because there is no server of ours.

  • No account, ever

    No sign-in, no email address, no profile. The only identifier is a random value generated on this device that is never sent anywhere.

  • Passwords are never saved

    Passwords, one-time codes and card security codes are discarded while the page is being read, before anything is written. Not masked, not encrypted — never stored.

  • One network request exists

    In the entire codebase, and only if you deploy the optional relay yourself. The build refuses to produce a package where that count is anything else.

  • No analytics of any kind

    No telemetry, no crash reporting, no tracking. Not in the extension, and not on this page — it loads no script and no external file.

  • No remote code

    Every script the extension runs ships inside the package. Nothing is fetched and executed later.

The one thing we do host

A share link needs a page for your recipient to open, and one is filled in by default so sharing works without you deploying anything. It is a static file. Both the encrypted answers and the key that opens them travel in the part of the link after the #, which browsers never send to a server — so that host receives a request for an HTML file and learns nothing about what is in the link. The extension itself never contacts it; only your recipient’s browser does.

You can point the setting at your own copy instead, or clear it and not share at all.
